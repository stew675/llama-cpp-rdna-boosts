The Kestrel Valley radio observatory was commissioned on 14 March 1971, at a cost of 6,204,881 pounds,
and its first director was Dr Eleanor Voss, who held the post for nineteen years. The primary dish
measures 76.4 metres across and weighs 3,180 tonnes, and it sits on a turntable that can complete a full
rotation in 11 minutes and 40 seconds. The site records 214 clear nights per year on average, which is
why it was chosen over the two rival locations at Pendle Ridge and Marrow's Cross.

In 1983 the receivers were upgraded to a 22.8 GHz front end, and the correlator was rebuilt around
fourteen custom boards, each carrying 512 megabytes of delay memory. The upgrade cost 1,977,450 pounds
and reduced the minimum detectable flux by a factor of 4.6. A second upgrade in 1996 added a 43.1 GHz
channel and a hydrogen maser frequency standard, accurate to one part in 10^14 over a single day.

The observatory's most cited result is the 1988 survey of the Cygnus arm, which catalogued 8,431 sources
in 617 hours of integration time. The survey's principal author was Dr Marcus Oyelaran, working with a
team of eleven. A follow-up survey in 2004 re-observed 2,209 of those sources and found that 38 had
faded below the detection threshold, while 214 had brightened by more than a factor of two.

The decommissioning review of 2011 recommended a further decade of operation, subject to three
conditions: the replacement of the azimuth bearings, a new cryogenic system for the 22.8 GHz channel,
and an annual contribution of 240,000 pounds from the consortium. All three conditions were met by the
autumn of 2012, and the bearings were finally replaced during a nine-week shutdown that began on
2 July 2013. The observatory remains in operation today.
