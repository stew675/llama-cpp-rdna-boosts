Distributed systems rely on consensus protocols to keep replicas consistent in the presence of failures. Raft decomposes the problem into leader election, log replication, and safety. A candidate increments its term, requests votes, and if it wins becomes leader for that term. Leaders append entries to their log and replicate them to followers, committing once a majority has stored the entry. Log matching and leader completeness guarantee that committed entries survive future elections. Learners and snapshots bound log growth. The practical difficulty is not the protocol but the implementation: clock assumptions, disk durability on commit, membership changes, and the interaction with the transport layer all introduce subtle bugs.

Memory allocators face a tension between speed, fragmentation, and locality. A bump allocator is fast but cannot reclaim individual objects. Free lists can reclaim but fragment. Segregated fit combines size classes with per-class free lists to keep allocation fast while limiting fragmentation. Thread caches avoid locks on the hot path but must be flushed to bound waste. Modern allocators also exploit huge pages and NUMA awareness. Measuring allocator quality requires workload-specific metrics: throughput, peak resident memory, tail latency under contention, and fragmentation after a long mixed workload.
