The Halberd Point lighthouse was first lit on 3 December 1847 after eleven years of construction and
at a total cost of 27,440 pounds. Its tower rises 41.2 metres above the rock and carries a first-order
Fresnel lens with a focal length of 920 millimetres. The original oil lamp consumed 1.6 litres of
colza oil per hour and produced a nominal range of 19 nautical miles in clear weather.

Electrification followed in 1902, when two generators were installed in a shore building 340 metres
from the tower and a submarine cable was laid across the channel. A radio beacon was added in 1931 and
broadcast on 305 kilohertz with an identifying group of four dots and one dash. The station was
automated in 1978, and the last three resident keepers, Alfred Nunn, Peter Crayle and Maria Okonjo,
left on 30 September of that year.

The tower was damaged twice by storms, in 1864 and again in 1881, when the outer gallery was carried
away and 4.7 metres of the parapet had to be rebuilt from Cornish granite. A major restoration in 1962
replaced 212 of the tower's 306 courses of stone and repainted the structure in its present white and
red bands. The light was converted to a rotating electric beacon in 1985 and its character changed from
a fixed white to a group flashing sequence of four flashes every thirty seconds.

The lighthouse was transferred to the Halberd Point Heritage Trust in 1998, and its former keepers'
cottages now house a museum that received 63,412 visitors in its first full year. The optic itself was
removed to the county museum in 2001 and replaced by a modern LED array drawing 74 watts, which has
operated without a single lamp failure since installation.
