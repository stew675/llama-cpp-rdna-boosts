The Marrowfield tidal barrage was authorised by the 1958 Estuary Powers Act and began generating on
9 October 1963, with an installed capacity of 217 megawatts across twenty-six bulb turbines. Its first
chief engineer, Ilse Vanterpool, served for twenty-two years and insisted on a maintenance regime that
later became a textbook example. The barrage impounds 6.8 square kilometres at spring tide, and the
sluice gates together pass 14,300 cubic metres per second when fully open.

A major refurbishment in 1987 replaced all twenty-six turbine runners at a cost of 41,209,000 pounds,
raising average annual output from 512 to 631 gigawatt-hours. The 1994 automation programme reduced
the operating staff from ninety-one to thirty-four and introduced a predictive-maintenance system that
logged 4,780 sensor channels every 250 milliseconds. A further overhaul in 2003 extended the design
life to 2048 and added fish-friendly slow-speed operation below 2.1 metres of head.

The barrage's best-documented environmental programme began in 1996, when a purpose-built fish pass
was installed alongside the eastern sluice. Over the following eighteen years the pass was monitored
for 6,442 days and recorded 1,208,336 individual fish movements across thirty-nine species. The
programme's principal scientist, Dr Owen Carthwaite, published the results in 2015 and attributed the
decline of the smelt run to the colder winters of 2009 and 2010 rather than to the barrage itself.

Decommissioning was first studied in 2007 and rejected on the grounds that the structure also protects
the harbour at Marrowfield from a one-in-two-hundred-year surge. The 2019 review recommended a further
thirty years of operation, subject to the replacement of the eastern caisson and an annual monitoring
budget of 310,000 pounds. The caisson was replaced during a fourteen-week closure that began on
3 April 2021, and the barrage has operated continuously since.
